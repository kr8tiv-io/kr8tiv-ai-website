What AI can and can't do for your Edmonton business

AI is a power tool, not a new hire. What it does well for Edmonton trades, shops and offices, where it falls on its face, and why firing people for a chatbot keeps backfiring.

By Matt Haynes, founder of KR8TIV AI · November 23, 2026 · 11 min read

The short answer

  • AI is very good at the repeatable work around the job: filing receipts, drafting quotes and emails, summarizing, sorting and flagging what slipped through the cracks.
  • AI is bad at being accountable, at knowing facts it was never given, and at judgment calls with an upset customer or a safety risk. It also makes things up with total confidence.
  • The research points one way: AI makes people faster, especially newer staff, while companies that cut staff for a chatbot (Klarna, Commonwealth Bank) have walked it back.
  • The winning setup for an Edmonton small business is AI doing the paperwork and people doing the work, the money calls and the customer.

AI can do a lot of the work around your work. It reads receipts, drafts quotes, writes the first version of the email you were dreading, summarizes the 40-minute call and notices the invoice nobody chased. What it can't do is be responsible, know things you never told it, or handle the moment where a customer is upset and the right answer is a human being.

So here is the whole article in one line: AI should help your people do a lot more, not replace them. The companies that tried the replacement plan are already walking it back, with apologies and rehiring announcements. Let them be the cautionary tale. You get to be the sequel.

AI is a power tool, not a new hire

Think of AI like the best intern you will ever meet. Tireless. Unreasonably fast. Trained on a mountain of text. Also occasionally, and with total confidence, completely wrong about things it has never seen.

You would not hand that intern the company chequebook, the safety sign-off or your angriest customer. You would hand them the stack of receipts, the first draft of the quote and the notes from Tuesday's site visit. Same rule for AI.

A nail gun did not replace the framer. It made one framer do the work of a slower crew, and the framer still decides where the nails go. That is the right mental model for AI in a small business: a power tool that multiplies the people you already trust.

What AI is genuinely good at

AI earns its keep on work that is repeated, rule-based and boring enough that people put it off until 10 PM.

Paperwork that repeats

Receipts, timesheets, expense categories, job logs, invoices. A photo of a gas station receipt can become a filed, categorized row in your books without anyone typing a thing. For a trades business, that is the difference between doing the books on Sunday night and not doing them at all.

First drafts of almost anything

Quotes, job reports, follow-up emails, safety meeting notes, job postings, review replies. AI writes the rough first version so your person spends their time on the part that needs judgment. A contractor can walk a job, dictate notes on the drive back and have a draft quote waiting at the next red light, checked against their real rates.

Reading, sorting and summarizing

The 60-page supplier agreement, the inbox that ate Monday, the recording of a two-hour planning meeting. AI can summarize, pull out the dates and dollar figures and tell you what needs an answer. You still decide what to do about it.

Catching the balls that drop

This is the quiet superpower, and it is most of what our automations do. A system that checks every morning for a deposit with no booked job, a quote nobody followed up on or a finished job that never got invoiced will save more money than any chatbot on your website.

Making new people good, faster

This one surprises owners. When Stanford and MIT researchers studied 5,179 customer support agents using an AI assistant, productivity rose 14% on average and 34% for novice and lower-skilled workers, while experienced agents saw little change. The AI was spreading the know-how of the best people to the newest ones. Customer sentiment improved and employees were more likely to stay. (Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, NBER / Quarterly Journal of Economics)

Read that again: the biggest win was making the people you already have better. Not removing them.

What AI can't do (so stop asking it to)

It can't be responsible

In 2024, Air Canada's website chatbot told a grieving customer they could claim a bereavement fare after travelling. That was wrong. When they asked for the money back, Air Canada argued the chatbot was responsible for its own actions. The BC Civil Resolution Tribunal called that "a remarkable submission" and ruled that the airline is responsible for everything on its website, "whether the information comes from a static page or a chatbot." (CBC News, Moffatt v. Air Canada, 2024 BCCRT 149)

Translation for an Edmonton business owner: if your AI promises a price, a date or a warranty, you promised it. A chatbot is not a separate legal entity. Air Canada checked.

It makes things up, confidently

AI models produce what OpenAI itself calls "plausible but false statements." OpenAI's own research says this happens partly because models are trained and graded in ways that reward a confident guess over "I don't know." (OpenAI, September 2025)

This is not theoretical. In Zhang v. Chen, a lawyer filed two court cases in a BC family law matter that ChatGPT had invented. They did not exist. The court ordered the lawyer to personally pay costs. (Zhang v. Chen, 2024 BCSC 285, summarized by Gardiner Roberts LLP)

If a lawyer can get burned, so can your quote, your spec sheet or your answer about whether that product is rated for -40.

It doesn't know your business unless you tell it

Ask a generic AI tool what you charge for a basement reno and it will happily guess. It does not know your rates, your suppliers, your warranty terms or the fact that you never take jobs in Leduc on Fridays. AI is only as good as the facts it has to work from. That is why we build a company brain first: one organized home for your prices, processes and voice, so the AI answers from your facts instead of the internet's vibes.

It's uneven, and it doesn't warn you

A Harvard Business School and Boston Consulting Group experiment with 758 consultants found that on tasks inside AI's abilities, people using GPT-4 completed 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, with significantly better quality. But on a task picked to sit just outside what the AI could do, consultants using AI were 19% less likely to get the right answer than those working without it. The researchers call this the "jagged frontier." (Dell'Acqua et al., Harvard Business School)

The AI did not raise its hand and say "this one's beyond me." It just sounded right. A person who knows the work is the safety rail.

It can't read the room

An upset customer, a crew member who looks off on a safety walk, a client who says "fine" in a way that means anything but. Judgment, empathy and accountability are people work. AI can draft the apology. A person should send it.

Replacing people with AI keeps backfiring

Here is our position, stated plainly: the companies that treat AI as a reason to fire people will be the ones that struggle. It is a bet, and so far the evidence is backing it.

Klarna said in 2024 that its AI assistant was doing the work of 700 customer service agents. In May 2025, its CEO told Bloomberg the cost-cutting had gone too far: "As cost unfortunately seems to have been a too predominant evaluation factor when organizing this, what you end up having is lower quality." Klarna started recruiting humans again so customers can always reach a person. (Fortune, reporting Bloomberg)

Commonwealth Bank of Australia announced 45 customer service job cuts in July 2025 after introducing an AI voice bot. A month later it reversed the decision, apologized and admitted the roles were not redundant after all. Workers said call volumes had actually gone up. (ABC News)

The regret is measurable. In an Orgvue survey of 1,163 senior leaders at large organizations, 39% said they had made employees redundant because of AI. Of those, 55% admitted they made the wrong decisions about those redundancies. (Orgvue, April 2025)

The analysts see the rebound coming. Gartner predicts that by 2027, half of the companies that attributed headcount cuts to AI will rehire people for similar work under new job titles. Its October 2025 survey found only 20% of customer service leaders had actually reduced staff because of AI. (Gartner, February 2026) Forrester went further and predicted that half of AI-attributed layoffs would be quietly reversed in 2026. (Forrester)

And the counterexample is instructive. IBM's CEO said AI took over work done by a few hundred HR staff, yet IBM's total employment went up, because the savings were put into programmers and salespeople. (PYMNTS, reporting The Wall Street Journal) The automation paid for more people doing higher-value work. That is the enabler model, at enterprise scale.

For a 6-person Edmonton company, the math is even simpler. You do not have 700 agents to cut. You have six people who know your customers, your trucks and your weird old building. Every one of them is worth more with AI than without it.

What the numbers say about Canadian businesses

Statistics Canada asks Canadian businesses about AI every year. A few findings worth knowing:

  • Adoption is climbing fast. 19.2% of Canadian businesses used AI to produce goods or deliver services in the 12 months before the 2026 survey, triple the 6.1% from 2024. (Statistics Canada, June 2026)
  • Small businesses are in it too. Among businesses with 1 to 4 employees, 19.9% reported using AI. (Same source.)
  • Trades are early. Construction sat at 9.2%, one of the lowest rates of any industry. If you run a trades business in Edmonton, most of your competitors have not started yet. (Same source.)
  • Training, not firing. Of businesses using AI, 44.4% changed their training or staffing practices, and the most common change was training existing employees (32.0%). (Same source.)
  • Headcount mostly held. In the 2025 survey, 89.4% of businesses using AI reported no change in their number of employees. (Statistics Canada, June 2025)

The pattern in Canada so far is not robots replacing crews. It is businesses teaching the people they already have to use a new tool.

A real Edmonton example: one crew, zero office staff

Evolve Eco Blasting is an industrial surface-restoration company in Greater Edmonton: one crew, real equipment and nobody in an office. The blasting was never the problem. The paperwork was. Receipts got photographed at gas stations and lost in the camera roll. A quote took three evenings. The schedule lived in the owner's head and a pile of text threads.

We built them a custom business app on the spreadsheet and drive they already owned. Nothing was replaced. Now:

  • A photo of a receipt becomes a filed, categorized expense.
  • A job walk becomes a priced, branded quote the same day, checked against media, labour, fuel and overhead before it is sent.
  • Every job is tracked from lead to deposit to invoice, and watchdog rules flag a deposit with no booking or an invoice unpaid past seven days.
  • Safety hazard assessments live on the crew's phones and get signed off on site.
  • Supplier prices are logged and watched, so a price jump gets noticed before it eats the margin.
  • An early-morning digest lands before the crew rolls out, whether anyone asks for it or not.

Office staff before: zero. Office staff after: zero. Nobody was replaced. What changed is who does the paperwork at 11 PM, which is now nobody. Two decisions stay with a person on purpose: approving the quote and marking the invoice paid. The money calls stay human.

That is the enabler model at the size most Edmonton businesses actually are.

How to start with AI without firing anyone

  1. List what happens after hours. Every task someone does at night, on weekends or "when it's quiet" is a candidate. Receipts, quotes, follow-ups, scheduling, reports.
  2. Pick one boring, repeatable job. Not "transform the business." One job. Get it running and trusted before adding the next.
  3. Keep a person on money, safety and customers. AI drafts and flags. A person approves the quote, signs the safety form and talks to the unhappy client.
  4. Give AI your real facts. Prices, processes, warranty terms and voice in one place, so it stops guessing. This is what a company brain is for.
  5. Use the tools you already have. You probably do not need new software. You need your spreadsheets, email and calendar pulling in the same direction.
  6. Train your people. AI pays off when people know where it is strong and where it slips (remember the jagged frontier), and Canadian businesses using AI are already training their staff. Teach your team properly and they will find uses you never thought of.

Quick test: should AI do this job?

Task Let AI do or draft it Keep a person on it
Filing receipts and expenses Yes Spot-check monthly
Drafting a quote from job notes Yes Approve before it is sent
Answering "what are your hours?" Yes, from your own info Review the answers it gives
Handling a complaint or refund Draft only Yes, always
Safety sign-off Prepare the form Yes, always
Legal or tax advice Summarize documents Yes, with a professional
Chasing an unpaid invoice Flag it and draft the reminder Decide when to call

If the task is repeatable and a mistake is cheap to catch, let AI take it. If a mistake costs money, safety or trust, AI prepares and a person decides.

Frequently asked questions

What can AI actually do for a small business?

It takes the repeatable admin off your people: reading receipts into your books, drafting quotes, emails and job reports, summarizing calls and documents, answering routine questions from your own information, and flagging things that slipped, like an unpaid invoice or a quote nobody followed up on.

What can't AI do for my business?

It can't take responsibility, it can't know facts you never gave it, and it can't be trusted to be right every time, because language models still produce confident wrong answers. Keep a person on anything involving money, safety, legal commitments or an unhappy customer.

Will AI replace my employees?

It shouldn't, and the evidence says trying is a bad bet. In Statistics Canada's 2025 survey, 89.4% of Canadian businesses using AI reported no change in employment, and companies such as Klarna and Commonwealth Bank of Australia reversed course after leaning on AI instead of people. The payoff comes from giving your team their time back.

Is my business too small for AI?

No. Statistics Canada found that 19.9% of businesses with 1 to 4 employees used AI in the 12 months before its 2026 survey, about the same rate as businesses overall. Small teams often feel it first, because so much of the admin lands on one or two people.

How do I start using AI in my business?

List the jobs that happen after hours, pick one boring, repeatable task, and automate that on the tools you already use. Keep a person approving anything that touches money or customers, give the AI your real prices and processes to work from, and train your team so they use it well.
