Why AI training for employees matters for Canadian businesses

Your team is already using AI. Here is what the Canadian numbers say, what goes sideways when people wing it, and how Edmonton and Alberta businesses can train their crews without the snooze-fest.

By Matt Haynes, founder of KR8TIV AI · November 25, 2026 · 10 min read

The short answer

  • Canadian employees are adopting AI faster than their employers are training them: 51% use generative AI at work, 83% say they need stronger skills, and only 48% say their employer trains them enough (KPMG, 2025).
  • Untrained use brings real risks: personal information leaking under PIPEDA or Alberta PIPA, confident wrong answers your business is liable for, and staff who quit the tools because they feel overwhelming.
  • Trained teams get the upside: 79% of Canadian AI users report better productivity, and BDC found small businesses using AI were 24% more productive.
  • Alberta has a head start waiting: construction sits at 9.2% AI use, less than half the national 19.2% (Statistics Canada, 2026). Good training is hands-on, role-specific and fun enough to stick.

Your team is already using AI. Someone in accounting is drafting emails with ChatGPT. Someone in the field is asking a chatbot to explain a spec sheet. And at least one person is quietly hoping nobody ever finds out how their reports got so good, so fast.

None of that is a crisis. It means your people are curious and want to work smarter. The catch is that curiosity without training tends to produce messy results, privacy headaches and the occasional answer that is confident, beautifully formatted and completely made up.

This guide covers why AI training for employees is worth doing, what the Canadian numbers actually say, what can go sideways when people wing it, and how businesses in Edmonton, across Alberta and all over Canada can get started without turning the office into a sci-fi movie. Spoiler: the training that works best is the training people actually enjoy.

The AI skills gap in Canada is real (and measurable)

AI adoption in Canada is not creeping along anymore. It is jogging. Statistics Canada found that 12.2% of Canadian businesses used AI to produce goods or deliver services in the year leading up to the second quarter of 2025, double the 6.1% reported a year earlier (Statistics Canada, 2025). By the second quarter of 2026 that figure reached 19.2%, roughly triple the 2024 rate (Statistics Canada, 2026).

Employees are moving even faster than their bosses. KPMG's Generative AI Adoption Index 2025 found that 51% of Canadian employees now use generative AI at work, up from 22% in 2023 (KPMG Canada, 2025).

Here is the gap. In that same KPMG research:

  • 83% of employees say they need stronger AI skills.
  • 82% want training provided by their employer.
  • Only 48% feel their employer provides enough training to use AI well in their role.

KPMG also points to global research with the University of Melbourne that ranked Canada 44th out of 47 countries for AI training and literacy. We are great at hockey and apologizing. AI literacy, not yet.

Your team is bringing its own AI to work

Microsoft and LinkedIn's 2024 Work Trend Index surveyed 31,000 people in 31 countries. It found that 75% of knowledge workers use AI at work, 78% of those users bring their own AI tools, and only 39% had received AI training from their company (Microsoft, 2024). The report also found 52% of AI users are reluctant to admit they use it for their most important tasks.

So in plenty of workplaces, AI is the coworker nobody officially hired. Training gets it out of the shadows and into a place where you can actually manage it.

The risks of untrained AI use

AI tools are useful. They are also extremely good at sounding certain about things they invented thirty seconds ago. Without training, a few predictable problems show up.

Data privacy: PIPEDA and Alberta PIPA

When an employee pastes a client file, a payroll spreadsheet or a customer complaint into a public AI tool, that personal information may leave your control. In Canada, private sector privacy is covered by the federal Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act (PIPEDA). In Alberta, most private sector organizations fall under the provincial Personal Information Protection Act (PIPA), overseen by the Office of the Information and Privacy Commissioner of Alberta. Federally regulated businesses, like banks and airlines, generally fall under PIPEDA instead.

Canada's federal, provincial and territorial privacy regulators published joint principles for responsible, trustworthy and privacy-protective generative AI. They include knowing your legal authority for using personal information, limiting collection to what is necessary, and using de-identified data where you can. Your staff cannot follow rules nobody taught them. (This article is general information, not legal advice. For your specific situation, talk to a privacy professional, not a chatbot.)

Accuracy and liability

AI mistakes look polished, which is exactly what makes them sneaky. In Moffatt v. Air Canada, 2024 BCCRT 149, the BC Civil Resolution Tribunal held Air Canada responsible after its website chatbot gave a customer incorrect information about bereavement fares (CRT decision). The dollar amount was small. The lesson was not: your business owns what its AI tools tell people. "The robot said it" is not a defence.

Trained employees know to check sources, verify numbers and keep a human in the loop for anything going to a client, a regulator or a job site.

Confusion and burnout

Bad training can backfire too. KPMG reported that 40% of Canadian employees did not know what AI controls were in place at their organization, and 37% started using AI after training but stopped because it felt overwhelming (Strategy, 2025). That is a lot of people who sat through a session, nodded politely and went right back to doing things the long way.

The productivity upside

Now the fun part. KPMG found 79% of Canadian employees who use generative AI say it has improved their productivity, and 54% report saving 1 to 5 hours per week. About half of those people say they put that time toward higher-value work like planning and improving operations. (The other half presumably finally cleaned out their inbox. Also valuable.)

The Business Development Bank of Canada (BDC) found that only 30% of Canadian small and medium-sized businesses used AI in 2025, yet those that did were 24% more productive than those that did not (BDC, 2026). BDC's State of Entrepreneurship study adds that businesses get more value from AI when they have an adoption plan, train their teams and use good internal data (BDC, 2026).

Notice that "train their teams" sits right in the middle of that list. Buying the tool is the easy part. Owning a treadmill has never made anyone fit either.

What good employee AI training covers

A one-hour webinar where someone reads slides about "the future" while everyone answers email does not count. Useful corporate AI training in Canada should cover:

The basics, explained like a human

What AI tools are good at, what they are bad at, and why they sometimes invent things with a straight face. No jargon required.

Your company's rules

Which tools are approved, what information can never go into them, and who to ask when something feels off. This is where your AI use policy lives or dies.

Hands-on practice with real work

Prompting that fits each role. Estimators, admin staff, project managers, marketers and owners all use AI differently. Practising on your own (de-identified) documents beats generic examples about planning a birthday party every time.

Checking the output

Fact-checking, citing sources, spotting made-up numbers and knowing when AI should not be used at all.

Keeping up

AI tools change constantly, sometimes between Monday and Wednesday. Good training includes a plan for refreshers so your team is not stuck on last year's tricks.

Making it fun enough to stick

Remember those people who tried AI after training and quit because it felt overwhelming? A dry lecture is a great way to get there. In our experience, people learn faster when they are laughing, trying things out loud and seeing a result on their own screen in the first few minutes. Interactive sessions, live demos and good visuals do more for adoption than slide 47 of 60.

The Alberta and Edmonton angle

Alberta runs on construction, energy, the trades and professional services. These are industries where AI has a lot of room to help, and where some have been slower to pick it up. Statistics Canada's 2026 data showed AI use was highest in information and cultural industries (42.3% of businesses), finance and insurance (40.4%) and professional, scientific and technical services (32.4%). Construction came in at 9.2%, less than half the national rate and among the lowest of any industry, just ahead of wholesale trade (7.9%) and agriculture, forestry, fishing and hunting (4.5%) (Statistics Canada, 2026).

That is not a knock on construction. People who pour foundations and run crews in minus 30 have better things to do than read AI newsletters. It does mean there is a big head start waiting for the companies that get their teams comfortable first. BDC's 2026 research makes the same point: AI is less often a priority for construction and primary sector businesses, but it can still help with administration, planning and customer service.

For Edmonton and Alberta businesses, practical wins often look like:

  • Construction and trades: drafting quotes and scopes of work, summarizing specs and safety documents, turning rambling voice notes into tidy job-site reports.
  • Energy and industrial services: organizing procedures, summarizing long technical documents, preparing first drafts of reports for human review.
  • Professional services: research summaries, client emails, meeting notes and proposal first drafts, with strict rules around confidential client data.
  • Every small business: admin, scheduling, marketing copy and answering the same customer question for the hundredth time without sighing audibly.

The point is not replacing anyone. It is giving a crew lead, a bookkeeper or a project manager back a few hours a week for the parts of the job that actually need a human.

How to start AI training at your business

  1. Find out what people already use. Ask openly, with zero judgment. You will be surprised, possibly impressed.
  2. Write a simple AI use policy. Approved tools, banned data, and a review step for anything client-facing.
  3. Pick two or three real use cases. Start where time is leaking out, like reporting, quoting or email.
  4. Train by role, hands-on. Short sessions on real tasks beat one marathon lecture.
  5. Measure and adjust. Track hours saved and errors caught, then expand what works.
  6. Schedule refreshers. The tools will change. Your training should too.

Frequently asked questions

Why is AI training for employees important?

Most Canadian employees who use AI want better skills, but fewer than half feel their employer trains them enough (KPMG, 2025). Training improves productivity, reduces privacy and accuracy risks, and helps teams use AI tools consistently and safely.

Do Alberta businesses need to think about privacy law when using AI?

Yes. Most private sector organizations in Alberta are covered by the Personal Information Protection Act (PIPA), and federally regulated businesses generally fall under PIPEDA. Employees should know what personal information can and cannot be entered into AI tools.

What should corporate AI training in Canada include?

AI basics and limits, your company's AI use policy, hands-on practice with role-specific tasks, how to fact-check outputs, privacy rules under PIPEDA or provincial law, and a plan for ongoing refreshers.

How long does it take to train a team on AI tools?

A focused seminar can cover the basics and company rules in a day. Building real habits usually takes hands-on follow-up sessions over several weeks, applied to each team's actual work.

Is AI training worth it for small businesses?

BDC found Canadian small and medium-sized businesses that used AI in 2025 were 24% more productive than those that did not, and that businesses get more value from AI when they train their teams.
